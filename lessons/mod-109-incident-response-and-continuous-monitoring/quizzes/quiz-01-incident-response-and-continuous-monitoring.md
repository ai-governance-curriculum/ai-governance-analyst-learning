## Questions

### 1. The analyst's stance and the three tangible outputs

Chapter 01 names the analyst's stance across this module and the artefacts the rest of the module reduces to. Which statement captures the chapter's framing?

- A. The analyst's stance is *incident commander and monitoring architect* — the role runs technical containment during an incident and designs the drift / quality / safety / cost panels the enterprise reads against, with the three tangible outputs being the containment plan, the dashboard design, and the Article 73 submission.
- B. The analyst's stance is *recorder, coordinator, and packager* — the role recognises the AI-specific incident class, logs the `INC-<year>-<sequence>` record against the register and the affected inventory row, coordinates the impact-assessment re-open, reads the continuous-monitoring dashboards for governance sign-off, escalates the signals that warrant it, drafts the Article 72 post-market surveillance summary and the Article 73 serious-incident notification, and packages the post-incident evidence for the governance council or the internal auditor. The three tangible outputs are the incident record, the monitoring-reading note, and the post-market surveillance summary (with the standing serious-incident-notification draft); the post-incident evidence pack is a format for delivering the incident record to a specific audience, not a fourth tangible output.
- C. The analyst's stance is interchangeable with `ai-eval-engineer` — the two roles co-own the dashboards, the thresholds, the alerting rules, and the monitoring-reading note; the chapter's *reading vs designing* distinction is a drafting-era artefact.
- D. The analyst's stance is *regulator-facing signer* — the role signs and submits the serious-incident notification to the market surveillance authority, with the chapter's references to an *accountable executive* describing the analyst's own executive authority on EU AI Act filings.

### 2. The six AI-specific incident classes and the jailbreak-vs-prompt-injection distinction

Chapter 02 fixes the taxonomy the analyst's recognition verb depends on. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. There are three AI-specific classes (regression, jailbreak, hallucination) and three general-software classes (outage, deployment, security); the analyst picks one of the six per incident with no *both-at-once* option.
- B. There are six AI-specific incident classes — model regression, jailbreak, prompt-injection exploitation, biased-outcome escalation, hallucination-driven harm, unauthorised model use — and the analyst sets severity at intake based on the apparent blast radius so the receiving specialist can triage faster.
- C. There are six AI-specific incident classes — model regression, jailbreak, prompt-injection exploitation, biased-outcome escalation, hallucination-driven harm, unauthorised model use — distinguished at intake by the analyst against a recognition rule (*an AI incident is one where the AI component's specific behaviour is what makes the event worth recording separately*). Jailbreak targets the *safety policy* layer (bypassing safety training or guardrails to produce prohibited content); prompt-injection exploitation targets the *instruction hierarchy* (untrusted input in the model's context causes the model to take an unauthorised action). The two frequently co-occur but are separate classes at intake; when in doubt, file both class fields and let `ai-infra-security` triage. Severity is *unset* at intake — the receiving specialist sets it against their taxonomy; the *both-at-once* pattern pairs an AI incident record with a classical software record and is common enough that the intake question is *is it one, or the other, or both*.
- D. Jailbreak and prompt-injection exploitation are the same class under a different name; the chapter deprecates the distinction in favour of the OWASP LLM01 label, and the analyst files any input-manipulation incident under a single *LLM01 tag* with the specific sub-shape recorded in the analyst note.

### 3. The `INC-<year>-<sequence>` row shape and the cross-reference discipline

Chapter 03 fixes the incident record's shape across the intake → triage → post-mortem cadence. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. The record is opened at triage once the primary-route specialist has confirmed the class; intake is a scratch-pad that does not persist on the record. Severity is set at intake to satisfy the GRC platform's required field; the specialist's triage severity overwrites it. Cross-references are descriptions (*"the support copilot"*) because descriptions read more naturally than IDs and are easier for a council member to interpret.
- B. The record is opened at intake with the minimum viable field set (identifier, opened date, class per chapter 02, affected `INV-<year>-<sequence>`, surface of report, primary route); severity is *unset* at intake and lands at triage per the receiving specialist's own taxonomy; the cross-reference block names every affected `INV`, `RISK`, `IA`/`FRIA`/`DPIA`, `EVAL`, `CTL`, `VND`, and `PMS` artefact *by ID, not by description*, and the linkage is bidirectional (the analyst writes `INC-<year>-<sequence>` back to the inventory row's *incident history* field when the GRC platform's link table does not enforce it). The record is updated at triage with the specialist's confirmation, containment actions and timestamps, availability state, and IA / FRIA / DPIA re-open status; completed at post-mortem with the post-mortem identifier, the analyst-voice root-cause translation, corrective and preventive actions, and the control re-tests. Closure requires a one-paragraph closure rationale naming what was resolved, what was deferred, and why — silent closure is a fail.
- C. The record is a free-text post-mortem that the engineering team writes; the analyst's role is to attach the write-up to the inventory row at post-closure and no earlier. Reclassification of the AI-specific class is a silent in-place overwrite because reclassification history is an audit-trail concern that the GRC platform handles automatically.
- D. The record carries one incident per inventory row per quarter — multiple incidents against the same system within a quarter are consolidated into a single row for register-roll-up efficiency, and cross-references are deduplicated at the quarter boundary.

### 4. The IA / FRIA / DPIA re-open trigger and the coordination protocol

Chapter 04 fixes the trigger evaluation, the coordination protocol, and the sign-off routing. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. The analyst drafts the IA / FRIA / DPIA update from the incident record's *root cause* field — the assessment's original owner is only consulted if the update has material implications for personal data. Stacked updates sign off in alphabetical order (DPIA, FRIA, IA) because the corpus indexes them that way.
- B. The trigger evaluation is a single question — *did the incident reach a user?* If yes, re-open all three assessments; if no, re-open none. The analyst drafts the updates in parallel and sends them to `legal` for a single consolidated sign-off round.
- C. The trigger evaluation is a four-question walk — Q1 *did the incident falsify a load-bearing claim the assessment relied on?*, Q2 *did it affect a population the assessment did not enumerate?*, Q3 *did it introduce a harm class the assessment did not consider?*, Q4 *did it render a measure-section commitment inoperative?* A *yes* on any one triggers the re-open for that assessment; a *no* on all four is a legitimate *not-triggered with rationale* outcome. The analyst *coordinates* (convenes the update round with the original owner and accountable executive; drives the timeline; routes for sign-off; records the outcome on the incident record, the assessment corpus with a versioned prior, the register, the system / model card, and the PMS record for high-risk systems); the assessment's original owner *drafts*. Sign-off routing differs by assessment type (IA → business / product accountable executive; FRIA → Article 27 accountable executive; DPIA → controller's accountable executive with DPO consulted and Article 36 consultation on residual high risk). Stacked updates convene once and sign off in **DPIA → FRIA → IA** order — the DPIA first because Article 36 consultation may affect whether processing can continue at all, the FRIA next because Article 27 registration may be affected, the IA last because its *residual risk* section reads the outputs of the other two.
- D. Stacked updates sign off in IA → FRIA → DPIA order; the IA goes first because the business ownership is clearer, and the DPIA is a formality once the IA is signed.

### 5. The four monitoring panel families and the analyst-tier reading depth

Chapter 05 fixes the panel-family taxonomy and the reading discipline. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. Continuous monitoring is organised around two panel families (quality and cost); drift and safety are specialist-only concerns that the analyst defers to `ai-eval-engineer` without reading. The monitoring-reading note is a quarterly artefact that the analyst composes from the alert history, not from reading the panels.
- B. Continuous monitoring is organised around four panel families — *drift* (data / concept / label / prediction), *quality* (task-appropriate accuracy signals), *safety* (guardrail / refusal / agent-tool / user-report signals), and *cost* (per-request / per-outcome / traffic-mix signals; cost is *governance-adjacent*, not a governance signal in itself). Analyst-tier reading depth is *read the panel against the referenced assessments (IA / FRIA / DPIA), register-entry residual claims, eval-evidence baseline, and system-card claims* — the analyst does *not* choose the metric, tune the threshold, set the alerting rule, perform root-cause analysis, or commit to a fix (those defer to `ai-eval-engineer` / `ai-infra-mlops` / the receiving specialist). The two things the analyst *does* on the panels themselves are (i) flag un-readable panels (missing axes, no threshold, no sampling-window label) as `mod-108` control-testing findings and (ii) flag missing panels where an IA / FRIA *measure* has no operating evidence. Cross-panel reading — drift-up + quality-down, quality-down + safety-up, safety-up + cost-up, cost-down + quality-down, cost-up + drift-up with quality-stable — is the discipline that distinguishes competent dashboard reading from panel-in-isolation reading. The monitoring-reading note is monthly per live AI system (plus on-trigger), with a fixed shape of header, four-family paragraphs, governance interpretation at one of three levels (continued sign-off / warning / escalation), and attachments.
- C. The four panel families are correct but the analyst's reading depth extends to *judging whether the specific metric value is good enough for the enterprise's risk appetite*; this judgement is the analyst's unique contribution and should not be deferred.
- D. Cost is a security signal because an unexplained cost spike indicates a coordinated attack by default; the analyst files every cost spike as a Class 2 security escalation immediately.

### 6. Monitoring-signal escalation classes and the Class D triggers

Chapter 06 fixes the escalation classes for monitoring signals and the rules for when each applies. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. Monitoring escalations route to a single owner (`head-of-ai-governance`) regardless of signal type, because the enterprise's governance queue is the common integration point. The 3× median rule is a severity label the analyst assigns, not a spike-detection heuristic.
- B. Monitoring escalations route under four classes — *A* (`ai-risk-engineer`, model-behaviour), *B* (`ai-infra-security`, security-adjacent), *C* (`ai-infra-mlops`, monitoring redesign / infrastructure), *D* (`head-of-ai-governance`, enterprise-level). The analyst files under the class whose trigger matches the signal (per the panel-family trigger tables); cross-panel escalations file under the panel carrying the strongest signal with the other affected classes *copied*. The *3× median rule* — *exceeds 3× the trailing 12-month median* — is a starting-point spike-detection heuristic that `ai-eval-engineer` / `ai-infra-mlops` may override with an enterprise-specific threshold; new systems (under 12 months in operation) use a shorter reference window. Class D applies specifically on four signal types: fundamental-rights-adjacent (segment thresholds cited in a FRIA, biased-outcome escalations reaching the trust-and-safety threshold, Article 73-adjacent); cross-system pattern (the same signal shape on three or more inventory rows in a reading period); council-agenda (signals informing a pending policy decision or a material assurance-posture change); executive-communication (signals that may require executive communication to customers, partners, regulators, or the board). Over-use of Class D (copying `head-of-ai-governance` on every escalation) dilutes the executive's queue and is a chapter-06 failure mode; under-use on fundamental-rights signals is worse. Resolution requires a resolving artefact identifier (a new eval, an incident record, a control record, a council-minute reference) — verbal commitments do not close escalations.
- C. There are two monitoring-escalation classes (urgent and non-urgent) distinguished by the analyst's own severity judgement at filing; routing is resolved after the escalation owner reads the summary.
- D. The 3× median rule applies only to safety-panel escalations; drift, quality, and cost signals use a uniform 1.5× standard-deviation rule with no heuristic override permitted.

### 7. Article 73 serious-incident reporting deadlines and the clock discipline

Chapter 07 fixes the EU AI Act Article 73 obligations at analyst-tier reading depth. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. Article 73 sets a single 30-working-day deadline from incident closure for all serious-incident notifications; the clock runs in working days per the Commission's implementing act. The analyst determines whether an incident is a *serious incident* from the incident record and submits the notification directly to the market surveillance authority once legal has reviewed the wording.
- B. Article 73 sets three deadline paths — a general **15-calendar-day** path from the moment the provider (or, where applicable, the deployer) becomes aware of the serious incident and establishes a causal link (or the reasonable likelihood of one); a **10-calendar-day** path in the case of the death of a person, running from the first *suspicion* of a causal relationship between the AI system and the serious incident; and a **2-calendar-day** path for a widespread infringement or a serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure. Deadlines are measured in calendar days, not working days; an initial, incomplete report followed by a complete report is permitted where necessary to ensure timely reporting. The *serious incident* definition is Article 3(49) and spans four outcome classes: (a) death or serious harm to a person's health; (b) serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure; (c) infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights; (d) serious harm to property or the environment. The analyst *does not determine* that an incident is a serious incident — that is a legal determination made by `legal` and the enterprise's EU AI Act compliance function on the analyst's evidence; the analyst reads for the four Article 3(49) criteria at intake and triage, flags plausibly-implicated incidents for same-day executive loop-in, and opens the notification draft *in parallel* with the incident record so the clock does not silently consume days before the accountable executive knows it is running.
- C. Article 73 applies only to AI systems the Commission has specifically designated by name; the analyst looks up the system against a Commission-published list and files only if the list names it. No deadlines apply to other systems regardless of outcome class.
- D. The analyst signs and submits the Article 73 notification once the engineering team's post-mortem has landed; the accountable executive is informed at post-closure for audit-reference purposes but has no role in submission.

### 8. The provider-vs-deployer role split and the analyst's drafting line on Article 73

Chapter 07 fixes the provider-vs-deployer obligation split and the analyst's drafting role on serious-incident notifications. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. The obligations are identical for providers and deployers; chapter 07's references to a *role split* describe a historical artefact of the Act's drafting that has since been collapsed by the Commission's implementing acts. The analyst records the same obligations on every inventory row regardless of whether the enterprise placed the system on the market.
- B. The enterprise is always a *deployer* on a hosted model provider's system because the model weights are the provider's; the Article 73 reporting obligation never attaches to the enterprise directly under any high-risk-system scenario.
- C. Provider obligations are more extensive — technical documentation, the Article 72 post-market monitoring plan and system, Article 73 serious-incident reporting, a quality management system, and continuous compliance with Chapter III Section 2. Deployer obligations under Article 26 include monitoring the operation of the high-risk system according to the instructions for use, immediately informing the provider (and, where applicable, the deployer's own market surveillance authority) of any serious incident, and — for the specified categories — completing a FRIA under Article 27. The enterprise's role is recorded on the inventory row and inherited onto the PMS record; systems where the enterprise is *both* carry both obligation sets. On the serious-incident notification itself, the **analyst drafts** per the chapter-07 notification-draft shape and **drives the deadline** against the calendar-day clock; the **accountable executive signs and submits**, with `legal` reviewing the substantive content. The analyst does not determine that an incident is a serious incident, does not sign the notification, does not submit the notification to the market surveillance authority, and does not negotiate the wording of regulator communication; parallel reporting under GDPR Article 33, MDR / IVDR, or other Union harmonisation legislation is run by the respective functions and *cross-referenced* on the Article 73 notification record so the enterprise can demonstrate coordinated handling.
- D. Providers never report serious incidents directly — the obligation always routes through the Member State's deployer-of-last-resort, and the analyst's draft is sent to that intermediary rather than to any market surveillance authority.

### 9. The post-incident evidence pack — its seven sections and audience tailoring

Chapter 08 fixes the pack's shape and how the council pack differs from the internal-audit pack. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. The pack has one section — a chronological narrative — and is composed before the post-mortem lands so that the council sees the incident while it is still fresh. The analyst assigns blame to the engineer or team whose change produced the incident, because governance readers need a clear accountability line.
- B. The pack has seven sections, in order: *§1 one-page summary* (what happened / when / what system / how surfaced / immediate impact / what the enterprise did / what changed / what remains open / what decision is being asked — hard one-page limit); *§2 timeline* (factual chronological table from awareness to closure, each row citing the underlying artefact); *§3 root-cause narrative in analyst voice* (translation of the engineering post-mortem into governance vocabulary, three questions — what condition made it possible, what triggered it, what allowed it to reach a user or downstream system — plus what the enterprise assumed that turned out false, per chapter 04's Q1–Q4); *§4 cross-reference walk* (a *reading* of the cross-reference block, not a duplicate — one paragraph per `INV`, `RISK`, `IA`/`FRIA`/`DPIA`, `EVAL`, `CTL`, `VND`, `PMS`); *§5 monitoring context* (before, during, after — the three monitoring-reading notes before the incident, the panel state at awareness, the two notes after); *§6 corrective and preventive actions* (table with status per action; corrective fixes the instance, preventive stops the class); *§7 analyst recommendation* (what to consider / what to recommend against / what the analyst commits to doing next — phrased as recommendation, not decision). The pack is a *reading*, not a duplicate — every claim cites the underlying artefact; it is dated and versioned (prior versions retained when the underlying state moves); it names its own limits; it stays at the systemic-condition altitude and does not translate technical error into individual blame (blame belongs in the engineering post-mortem if anywhere, not in the governance pack). The council pack and the internal-audit pack share substantive content; the council pack tailors §1 and §7 for an enterprise-level reader and emphasises §4's register and assessment interactions; the internal-audit pack goes deeper on §5 and §6 because internal audit will re-test the corrective / preventive action claims and read the monitoring-reading notes as evidence of continuous-monitoring operation.
- C. The pack has three sections — summary, root cause, and recommendation — and the council and internal-audit audiences receive identical copies. Dating and versioning are optional because the incident record is the versioned artefact of record.
- D. The pack's §3 root-cause narrative is authored by the engineering team and copied verbatim into the pack; the analyst's role is to attach the engineering write-up without translation, so that the technical precision is preserved for the council reader.

### 10. The four module-level escalation classes and the delegation boundary

Chapter 09 fixes the delegation-boundary map and the four escalation classes the analyst uses when work outgrows the analyst's scope. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. There are no distinct escalation classes in this module — every escalation routes to `head-of-ai-governance` via the same email channel, and the register entries are flat text with no class or module tagging. The analyst may take a residual-risk-acceptance decision on behalf of the risk owner if the owner is unavailable at the moment the decision is needed.
- B. There are four module-level escalation classes — **Class 1 incident-command handoff** (an incident that grows past the analyst's coordinating scope — customer-facing disclosure needed, regulator has contacted the enterprise, cross-business-unit blast radius; routes to `head-of-ai-governance` with the enterprise's crisis-management function copied; analyst supplies the evidence pack or an interim brief and does not take customer-disclosure, regulator-engagement, or executive-sign-off-containment decisions); **Class 2 monitoring redesign** (a monitoring gap that recurs across incidents or periods — same panel repeatedly un-readable, same signal repeatedly missed, same threshold repeatedly miscalibrated, category of signal the enterprise needs to observe not observable; routes to `ai-infra-mlops` and `ai-eval-engineer` with `senior-ai-governance-architect` for the library-level implication; analyst supplies the pattern and the evidence, does not design the new panel, choose the metric, set the threshold, or write the alerting rule); **Class 3 regulatory filing** (incident meets the Article 3(49) threshold or an equivalent under GDPR / MDR / IVDR / other Union harmonisation legislation; routes to the accountable executive per the enterprise's EU AI Act compliance function with `legal` and — where personal data is in scope — `data-protection-officer` copied; analyst supplies the notification draft, the Article 3(49) walk, and the calendar-day countdown, does not determine the incident is a serious incident, sign the notification, submit it, or negotiate regulator-communication wording); **Class 4 governance-council decision** (an enterprise-wide policy change, a residual-risk acceptance beyond a named threshold, a decision on whether to continue an incident-affected system in operation, a public-disclosure decision; routes to the governance council convened by `head-of-ai-governance`; analyst supplies the full evidence pack, the `head-of-ai-governance` recommendation, the specific decision being asked, and timing constraints — does not take the decision, withdraw the escalation because a downstream role promised remediation, or downgrade the class to avoid the council slot). Escalations share the `ESC-<year>-<sequence>` register with `mod-108` with a *module* field distinguishing this module's entries and the four-class taxonomy specific to it; the **Article 73 clock starts on awareness, not on triage** — the notification draft opens in parallel with the incident record and the accountable executive is looped in same-day for possible-serious-incident cases; pattern-recognition rules turn recurring incidents (three of the same class on one row within a rolling quarter; three of the same class across different rows within a quarter; repeated Class B monitoring escalations on jailbreak / prompt-injection signals; repeated Class 3 filings concentrated in one system; Class 4 escalations that resolve without decisions) into signals for `head-of-ai-governance` and `senior-ai-governance-architect` — the analyst raises the pattern, the owning roles set policy.
- C. The four escalation classes exist but map one-to-one with the four Article 3(49) outcome classes — Class 1 for death or serious harm, Class 2 for critical infrastructure, Class 3 for fundamental rights, Class 4 for property or environment — and the analyst files one class per incident based on the outcome class.
- D. Class 3 escalations are resolved when the analyst submits the notification; the accountable executive's role is limited to acknowledging receipt of the submission for audit-reference purposes.

## Scoring
