## Questions

### 1. The analyst's stance and the three tangible outputs

Chapter 01 names the analyst's stance across this module and the artefacts the module reduces to. Which statement captures the chapter's framing?

- A. The analyst's stance is *decision-maker, chair, ratifier, and enterprise spokesperson* — the role sits at the top of the AI governance council, ratifies the acceptable-use policy, signs regulator submissions, and speaks of record at media touch-points, with the three tangible outputs being the policy library, the regulator submission record, and the media-response record.
- B. The analyst's stance is *translator, meeting-packet packager, minutes-and-decisions recorder, and first-cut drafter* — translating technical artefacts into the receiving audience's language, assembling the council pre-read, recording minutes that survive audit, and originating first-cut policy and standard drafts that route for architect edits and head-of-governance ratification. The three tangible outputs are (i) the audience-adapted briefing, (ii) the council packet (pre-read deck + one-pager plus post-meeting minutes and action-item register), and (iii) the first-cut policy / standard draft. External-facing communication (chapter 07) and the certifications portfolio (chapter 08) are *disciplines* that modulate how the three families are written, not separate tangible outputs.
- C. The analyst's stance is interchangeable with `senior-ai-governance-architect` — the two roles co-own first-cut drafting, consultation, edits, and ratification, with the chapter's references to a *routing chain* describing a pre-2024 drafting-era artefact that has since been collapsed.
- D. The analyst's stance is *regulator-facing signer* — the role signs and submits regulator responses and external model cards under the EU AI Act Article 50 transparency regime, with the chapter's references to `head-of-ai-governance` describing the analyst's own executive authority on external-facing submissions.

### 2. The translation-not-summarisation rule and per-audience structure

Chapter 02 fixes the discipline that separates an analyst-tier briefing from a marketing document. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. Translation is a synonym for summarisation; the point of the briefing is to compress the underlying artefact so the audience has less to read, and omitting the metric's confidence interval or the incident's affected-user count is appropriate when it would lengthen the briefing.
- B. Translation is a synonym for rewriting; the analyst re-pitches the underlying claim in the audience's words even when that extends the claim beyond what the eval, incident record, or register entry supports — e.g., writing *"the model is unbiased"* when the eval measured demographic parity on one axis and found a small difference.
- C. Translation is the restatement of what the artefact claims in the receiving audience's language *without changing the claim*. Two rules bound it: every claim in the briefing traces to an underlying artefact ID (`EVAL-`, `INC-`, `RISK-`, `IA-`, `CTL-`, etc.) — no traceability, no briefing; and the briefing does not add claims the artefact does not support — where the audience's decision requires a claim the artefact lacks, the analyst names the gap explicitly and routes it rather than filling it in. The briefing's structure follows the audience's decision (legal reads for exposure, privacy for lawful basis and rights, security for threat class and exploit path, product for the launch gate, executive committee for portfolio-level decisions), and one briefing is produced per audience even when the underlying artefact is the same.
- D. The analyst writes one briefing per underlying artefact and sends the same briefing to every audience that needs to act on it; per-audience adaptation is the receiving role's responsibility once the briefing lands in their inbox.

### 3. ISO/IEC 38507 and the governance-body-vs-management distinction

Chapter 03 fixes the standard's role in the analyst's board-facing work. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. ISO/IEC 38507:2022 is a certifiable AI management-system standard with a *shall / should / may* clause structure and an accompanying audit standard; the enterprise can be certified against it and the analyst cites *certified against 38507* on the trust site as a compliance claim.
- B. ISO/IEC 38507:2022 is a guidance standard — not certifiable, not a source of compliance obligations — that fixes the vocabulary the analyst uses when drafting for the governance body (board, board committee, or a non-board accountable equivalent). It distinguishes the *governance body* (accountable for performance and conformance) from *management* (responsible for planning and executing); organises the governance body's role around the *evaluate / direct / monitor* triad inherited from the ISO/IEC 38500 family; and gives the analyst an AI-specific considerations vocabulary — *autonomy, opacity, data use, cultural and behavioural change, third-party dependence* — to lift verbatim into the briefing. Every material claim in a board-facing draft is attributable to either the board (past decisions, endorsed appetite, ratified policies) or management (current operations, ongoing work, upcoming asks); claims attributable to neither are the single most common failure mode. The analyst drafts; `head-of-ai-governance` or the named accountable executive delivers.
- C. ISO/IEC 38507 and ISO/IEC 42001 are interchangeable; the analyst picks whichever has the shorter vocabulary for a given briefing, and the governance-body-vs-management distinction is a presentational preference rather than a load-bearing attribution rule.
- D. The AI governance council is a *governance body* under 38507, so briefings written for the council use the full 38507 vocabulary and the board inherits the same altitude of discussion from the council's minutes without a distinct briefing.

### 4. The council pre-read: standing agenda, deck + one-pager, decisions-needed slide, cadence

Chapter 04 fixes the pre-read's shape and cadence. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. The pre-read is a single long deck (typically 50+ slides) delivered on the morning of the meeting so the content is fresh; the one-pager is deprecated in favour of the deck-only pattern; and the decisions-needed slide is appended only when the chair requests one, because most council decisions are reducible to a yes/no vote.
- B. The pre-read is a *standing agenda + delta* package — a deck (one slide per agenda item, where each slide's title is the decision or discussion question, with every claim traced to an artefact ID and speaker notes carrying the depth) and a one-pager (the single-page written summary a member with ten minutes reads: portfolio-at-a-glance, since-last-meeting bullets, decisions-the-council-is-asked-to-take-today, for-information items, escalations pending, cross-references). The *decisions needed this meeting* slide appears twice in the deck (once early so members know what the meeting is working toward, once at the end so the chair can walk the council through the votes at close) and lists each decision with a *recommending role* and an *option set* (not a yes/no reduction) confirmed with the chair ahead of time — surprise decisions belong on the *for information* list and are escalated to the *decisions* list at the *next* meeting. The cadence runs M − 14 (standing-agenda draft to chair) → M − 10 (contributors notified) → M − 7 (content due) → M − 5 (first-draft deck + one-pager to chair) → M − 3 (chair-approved pack circulated with the invitation) → M (meeting) → M + 1 (draft minutes to chair) → M + 3 (approved minutes circulated) → M + 5 (action-item register updated). Slipping a pre-meeting step lands the pack cold; slipping a post-meeting step evaporates the action items. The analyst's per-cycle work is roll-up and delta computation from the earlier-module artefacts, not fresh authoring; where the content is not derivable, that is itself a finding to file back to the earlier-module owner rather than fill in by hand.
- C. Sensitive items (an in-flight legal-privileged matter, a pending regulator submission, a personnel matter, market-sensitive commercial information) are fully detailed on the circulated pre-read because the council needs the substance ahead of time; the analyst decides unilaterally which items qualify.
- D. The standing agenda is re-ordered each month to foreground the most-interesting items, so members read the pack fresh each cycle and spot the critical discussion items quickly; consistency of shape is a presentational preference, not a load-bearing feature.

### 5. The five-field decision-record pattern and the audit-survival test

Chapter 05 fixes what makes council minutes survive audit. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. Minutes are a verbatim transcript of the meeting discussion including tone-of-meeting notes (*"this discussion was heated"*, *"the presenter did a good job"*); the discussion flow is the audit-load-bearing content and attribution is left loose to protect member confidentiality.
- B. Minutes are composed days after the meeting from the analyst's free-text notes, with decisions reconstructed after the fact; the analyst edits the ratified minutes directly when a member later spots an error, because version control is handled by the GRC platform's edit history.
- C. Every council decision reduces to five fields on the minute: (i) *decision ID* (`DEC-<year>-<sequence>` or an equivalent agenda-item-plus-meeting-date handle); (ii) *artefact scope* (the specific artefact ID(s) the decision is about); (iii) *decision text* (the exact option chosen from the pre-read's option set); (iv) *vote outcome* (unanimous / majority with N in favour, M against, K abstaining / chair's decision where terms of reference permit); (v) *conditions and dissent* (numbered conditions each with owner and due date, dissent captured verbatim or paraphrased with the dissenting member's permission and attributed by named role). Attribution is on-the-record — un-attributed dissent (*"there was some concern"*) is an integrity failure. Language discipline is third person, past tense, active voice; verbatim quotation is reserved for recorded dissent and the specific-conditions text that was voted on. Once ratified at the next meeting a minute is *frozen* — corrections follow the enterprise's records-correction process (typically a chair-signed correction appendix), never silent edits. The audit-survival test the analyst runs before circulating: *if an auditor reads only these minutes, can they reconstruct what was decided, by whom, on what basis, and what happened next?*
- D. Approved-with-conditions decisions record the conditions as a free-text paragraph without numbered conditions, named owners, or due dates, because the follow-through lives on the action-item register and the minute need not duplicate it.

### 6. Policy vs standard vs guidance vs procedure, and the four-phase routing chain

Chapter 06 fixes the level hierarchy and the drafting-through-ratification path. Which statement captures the chapter?

- A. Policy, standard, guidance, and procedure are interchangeable names for the same artefact at different lengths; the analyst drafts a *first-cut* at whatever length the content needs and `head-of-ai-governance` decides whether the enterprise calls it a policy or a standard at ratification.
- B. The enterprise's policy library organises on four levels — *policy* (enterprise-wide statement of intent, 1–5 pages, ratified by the board or a C-level, audience *all* employees / systems / vendors); *standard* (normative operational requirement, 5–20 pages, ratified by `head-of-ai-governance` or the equivalent, audience named practitioner roles); *guidance* (non-normative recommendation in plain language, 2–10 pages, lighter ratification, audience a named employee group); *procedure* (step-by-step operational instructions owned by the team that runs the tooling). The analyst originates first-cut drafts at policy, standard, and guidance levels; procedures typically belong to the team that runs the underlying tooling. First-cut drafts move through a four-phase routing chain — (i) *first-cut draft* (`ai-governance-analyst`); (ii) *architect edits* (`senior-ai-governance-architect`); (iii) *cross-functional consultation* (architect runs it with named `legal` / `privacy` / `security` / `HR` / `procurement` / business-line contributors); (iv) *ratification* (`head-of-ai-governance` or the enterprise's policy-approval-workflow authority) — tracked on a *routing header* at the top of the document that names the routing state, author, architect editor, consultation contributors with status, ratifier, supersession, effective date, and next scheduled review. The universal section shape (purpose / scope / definitions / requirements or recommendations / roles / exceptions and waivers / enforcement or effect / related documents / revision history) applies to every analyst-originated draft. Four canonical first-cut drafts sit at this origination tier: the **acceptable-use policy** (policy level; prohibited-use list mirrors EU AI Act Article 5), the **model-approval standard** (standard level; operationalises intake → tiering → risk → fairness → IA → eval → controls → council sign-off → post-deployment monitoring), the **third-party AI vendor review standard** (standard level; closes the free-tier / trial-vendor gap explicitly), and the **generative-AI employee guidance** (guidance level; plain language, non-normative). Level confusion (writing procedure detail — specific tool names, version numbers, configuration settings — into an AUP, or writing only high-altitude intent into a standard) is the most common first-cut failure.
- C. The routing chain has two phases — first-cut draft by the analyst, then ratification by `head-of-ai-governance` — with architect edits and cross-functional consultation treated as optional shortcuts the analyst can skip when the draft is time-pressured, because `legal` and `privacy` review the ratified version after publication and raise edits in-place.
- D. The AUP's prohibited-use list is left to the enterprise's own discretion — mirroring EU AI Act Article 5 in the prohibited-use section is treated as marketing content rather than policy content, and enterprise-specific additions on top of Article 5 are discouraged.

### 7. External-facing responsible-AI communication and the public-facing vocabulary discipline

Chapter 07 fixes the four external artefact families, the review chain, and the vocabulary rules. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. External-facing responsible-AI content is drafted, signed, and published by the analyst from the analyst's own account — the analyst speaks of record for the enterprise's AI posture, responds directly to media inquiries, submits regulator responses under their own name, and resolves a material mis-statement in a previously-published trust-site page by silently editing the page in place.
- B. Four external-facing artefact families sit at the analyst's contribution altitude — (i) *trust site content* (plain-language summary of the enterprise's AI governance posture; analyst drafts and factual-reviews, `communications` publishes); (ii) *published model cards* (external version derived from the internal `mod-105` card, reconciled so the external claim never outstrips the internal evidence and never omits limitations the internal card names; analyst drafts, `senior-ai-governance-architect` owns the published shape); (iii) *regulator responses* (routine and non-routine; analyst drafts and fact-checks the factual content, `legal` reviews, `head-of-ai-governance` approves, the accountable executive named for the regulator and jurisdiction signs and submits); (iv) *public consultation responses* (analyst drafts factual content; `head-of-ai-governance` or an executive takes the position). Vocabulary discipline is strict: framework names are precise with versions (*ISO/IEC 42001:2023*, *NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0*, *Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the EU AI Act)*); verbs of adherence are distinguished — *certified* (an accredited certification body has issued a certificate) ≠ *aligned* (practice tracks the framework but there is no third-party attestation) ≠ *implementing* (in progress) ≠ *compliant* (statute-flavoured; usually avoided outside legal review); metrics are cited with their measurement basis and date; limitations are named; no comparative claims about competitors; no speculation about future capability. EU AI Act Article 50 transparency notices (interaction-is-AI disclosure, deepfake / synthetic-content labelling, emotion-recognition / biometric-categorisation notification subject to the article's exceptions) are *compliance artefacts*, not marketing content — short, factual, in the language of the affected individual's jurisdiction, readily accessible. A material mis-statement in an already-published external artefact, an unreviewed regulator inquiry, a press or media inquiry, a whistleblower report received externally, and any ask to publish before the review chain is complete are all *stop and escalate* signals routed to `legal` and `head-of-ai-governance` (and `communications` where the exposure is public-facing) — the analyst does not silently fix.
- C. *Certified* and *aligned* are synonyms the analyst uses interchangeably, so *"our systems meet ISO/IEC 42001"* on the trust site is supportable as long as the enterprise's practice tracks the standard, even in the absence of an accredited certification body's certificate.
- D. Published model cards are the external copy of the internal model card, verbatim, with internal system identifiers and internal team names preserved for transparency; reconciliation against eval-evidence rows (`mod-107`) and limitations named in the internal card is a *nice-to-have* rather than a load-bearing step.

### 8. Certifications: AIGP first, depth extensions second, peer-role credentials never

Chapter 08 positions the analyst's professional-development portfolio. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. The analyst sequences peer-role credentials first (CISSP, CISM, AWS Machine Learning Specialty) to demonstrate technical breadth before pursuing the AIGP; credential-collecting across every adjacent domain is the analyst's differentiator on the hiring market.
- B. The IAPP AIGP is pursued only after the analyst has held at least five years in role; before that the analyst relies on the enterprise's internal shared vocabulary without an external credential anchor.
- C. The IAPP AIGP is the analyst's *shared communication vocabulary* with cross-functional peers and is pursued first — the AIGP body of knowledge is the single best proxy for the AI-governance community's converging vocabulary, maps directly to the track's `mod-101` / `mod-103` / `mod-105` / `mod-106` / `mod-108` material, and sits at the analyst-through-senior-manager altitude the role occupies. CIPP/E or CIPP/US is sequenced *after* AIGP as the privacy-adjacent depth extension, chosen by the enterprise's jurisdictional footprint (CIPP/E for EU / EEA exposure; CIPP/US for US-centric portfolios with consumer or worker data; both for cross-Atlantic enterprises, sequenced with a year or two between them). ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer / lead-auditor is the standards-and-assurance depth extension, pursued particularly on the senior-architect promotion path. Peer-role credentials (CISSP / CISM / CISA from ISC² and ISACA; PMP / Prince2; AWS ML Specialty / Google Professional ML Engineer; vendor-specific GRC or responsible-AI certifications; PhD-level academic credentials) belong to peer roles and are *not* on the analyst's default plan — the analyst borrows their vocabulary without competing on their exams. Credential-plus-practice is the discipline — three credentials over three years is a reasonable pace; more is a credential-collecting signal — and the written portfolio plan is reviewed annually with the analyst's manager.
- D. The ISO/IEC 42001 lead-implementer credential certifies the analyst's enterprise against the standard directly; the individual holder's training is equivalent to the enterprise's conformity assessment and replaces the need for an accredited certification body.

### 9. The AUP's prohibited-use list and the exception process

Chapter 06's acceptable-use policy first-cut carries specific anchor content. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. The AUP is 15–20 pages long and names approved tools by product name and version so employees have an authoritative list in the policy document itself; the exception process is optional and is added only if employees ask for one.
- B. The AUP is 3–5 pages long (policies longer than this stop being read) and names *categories* of use rather than specific tools (the approved-tools list lives in the `mod-102` inventory, not in the policy). The prohibited-use list mirrors EU AI Act Article 5's prohibitions (subliminal techniques materially distorting behaviour; exploitation of vulnerabilities of specific groups; social scoring by public authorities; real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces subject to the article's narrow law-enforcement exceptions; untargeted scraping to build facial-recognition databases; emotion recognition in the workplace or educational institutions subject to the article's exceptions; biometric categorisation on protected characteristics; predictive policing based solely on profiling; individual criminal-risk assessment based solely on profiling) with enterprise-specific prohibitions layered on top. Every policy carries an exception process — standard-level exceptions route through `senior-ai-governance-architect` for approval; policy-level exceptions route through `head-of-ai-governance` and escalate to the AI governance council for endorsement; exceptions are recorded as `EXC-<year>-<sequence>` in the GRC platform; no self-approved exceptions; the exception register is reviewed on the council standing agenda; and prohibited-use requirements are *not* subject to exception under the AUP (a use case apparently requiring an Article 5 exception routes separately through `legal` and `head-of-ai-governance` as a distinct policy-level decision, not as a routine AUP waiver). A policy with no path for exceptions gets ignored rather than followed.
- C. The AUP's prohibited-use list is composed from the enterprise's internal legacy IT acceptable-use policy; mirroring EU AI Act Article 5 in the AI acceptable-use policy is a chapter-06 anti-pattern because statutes should not appear in internal policy documents.
- D. Standard-level exceptions and policy-level exceptions route through the same approver (the analyst), on the premise that the analyst has drafted the policy and therefore understands which exceptions are safe to grant.

### 10. The four module-level escalation classes and the recurring-pattern promotion rule

Chapter 09 fixes the delegation-boundary map and the escalation shapes work routes under when it outgrows the analyst's desk. Which statement matches the chapter?

- A. There is a single escalation route (email to `head-of-ai-governance`) for every situation; recurring patterns of the same escalation are handled by re-sending the email with a heavier cc list rather than by promoting the pattern to a policy-work item.
- B. Four escalation classes route module-level work — **Class 1 content-owner delay or non-response** (a risk / assessment / policy / control owner has not delivered a pre-read contribution or a consultation input by the required date and the analyst's follow-ups have not moved the item; routes to `senior-ai-governance-architect` for policy-and-standard consultation delays, or to `head-of-ai-governance` for council-pre-read contribution delays; brief names the item, owner, requested content and date, follow-ups made, and the downstream impact); **Class 2 chair / ratifier boundary-holding request** (the analyst is being pushed to publish outside the routing chain, record a decision the council did not take, omit dissent from a minute, bypass consultation, short-circuit ratification, or speak externally before legal review; the analyst declines and escalates to the chair or ratifier for confirmation of the boundary; a recurring ask-pattern across multiple askers is promoted to a class-4 policy-work item); **Class 3 regulator / external audit / material public exposure** (a regulator has opened an inquiry; an external audit has scoped an engagement; a public statement, media report, or third-party publication has raised material exposure; routes to `legal` and `head-of-ai-governance` simultaneously, `communications` where public-facing; a regulator or auditor contact that reaches the analyst directly is routed to `legal` and `head-of-ai-governance` *before* the analyst responds substantively — the analyst does not become the regulator's contact-of-record); **Class 4 recurring pattern → policy-work** (the analyst has seen the same class of problem across multiple meetings, systems, or content owners and the fix is not another instance of the same operational action but a change to policy, standard, training material, tooling configuration, or role-clarity statement; routes to `senior-ai-governance-architect` and `head-of-ai-governance`; the memo may itself land as a standing-agenda item at the next council). The recurring-pattern promotion rule is explicit: **second recurrence** — the analyst notes the pattern in the pre-read *for information*; **third recurrence** — the analyst promotes the pattern to a class-4 escalation with a proposed policy-work item; after promotion, individual instances continue to route as normal but a running instance-count on the escalation register tracks whether the policy-work item is closing the pattern. If the analyst is doing the same operational escalation every month, the analyst has missed the promotion moment.
- C. The four escalation classes exist but map one-to-one to the five day-to-day audiences from chapter 02 (legal / privacy / security / product / executive committee) — the analyst files one class per audience based on whose inbox the escalation lands in.
- D. Regulator or external-audit contact that reaches the analyst directly is handled by the analyst's own response first, with legal and head-of-ai-governance looped in only after the response has gone out, to preserve response speed.
